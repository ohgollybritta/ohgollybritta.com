The Project

Two patriots,
in their own right.

This project began with a straightforward genealogical question: does the documentation support Rebecca Bryan Boone and Jemima Boone Callaway as Patriot Ancestors of the National Society Daughters of the American Revolution, independent of the men to whom they are related? The NSDAR Female Patriot Ancestor designation is specific on this point. It requires documented service that stands on its own: service that does not rely on proximity to, or the record of, a male relative. For women on the 1778 Kentucky frontier, whose labor was rarely recorded under their own names, meeting that standard requires assembling primary and secondary sources with care.

Both Rebecca and Jemima meet it. The work builds on an earlier piece in Compass Magazine (The Boone Society) from July 2023, titled "The Eviza Coshow Letters." For America 250, that research was expanded into a broader treatment of Rebecca and Jemima as NSDAR Female Patriot Ancestors and delivered as a presentation to a local DAR chapter in March 2026.

America 250 (the commemoration of the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence) has been an occasion to re-examine whose contributions entered the founding narrative and whose did not. For the women at Fort Boonesborough, the documentation exists. It has simply not been foregrounded.

The patriot service of Rebecca and Jemima is documented in its own right, not borrowed from the notable men in their vicinity.
Eviza Lydia Coshow (née Howell), a granddaughter of Jemima Boone Callaway and primary source for the Draper Correspondence
The Patriots

The Female Patriots:

Rebecca Bryan Boone
January 9, 1739 – March 18, 1813
Wife of Daniel Boone and matriarch of the Boone household at Fort Boonesborough. A documented markswoman. During the 1778 siege she extinguished fires set by flaming arrows on the fort roof, molded ammunition, and maintained the food and medical stores over the eleven days of the assault. She and Daniel were reinterred at Frankfort Cemetery, Kentucky, in 1845.
Jemima Boone Callaway
October 4, 1762 – August 30, 1834
Daughter of Daniel and Rebecca Boone. Abducted by a Shawnee-Cherokee party in July 1776, at age thirteen, along with two Callaway sisters, and recovered two days later after the captives left deliberate trail markers for their rescuers. During the 1778 siege she left the fort after dark to gather spent musket balls, which were recast and returned to the defenders. Her 1776 abduction is traditionally cited as source material for James Fenimore Cooper's The Last of the Mohicans (1826).
Documented Service

Defenders of
Fort Boonesborough.

The Siege of Boonesborough began on September 7, 1778, and continued for eleven days. A Shawnee force under Chief Blackfish, operating in coordination with British interests on the frontier during the broader Revolutionary War, laid siege to the stockade at Boonesborough. It remains among the longest frontier engagements of the war.

Both women are documented defenders. Rebecca served as markswoman and ammunition supplier. Jemima's nightly ammunition-gathering is preserved in the Draper Manuscript Collection, through correspondence from her granddaughter Eviza Lydia Coshow to the historian Dr. Lyman Copeland Draper.

Rebecca: extinguished fires started by flaming arrows on the fort roof, molded bullets, and maintained food and medical stores across the siege.
Jemima: collected spent musket balls at night, which were then recast and returned to the defenders the following day.
Legacy: in 1943 a WWII Liberty ship, the SS Rebecca Boone, was named in Rebecca's honor.
Literature: Jemima's 1776 abduction is traditionally cited as source material for James Fenimore Cooper's The Last of the Mohicans (1826).
Art: the Boone family became subjects of nineteenth-century American paintings and engravings, including Karl Bodmer's 1852 Capture of the Daughters of Daniel Boone and Callaway by the Indians and Howard Pyle's 1887 siege illustrations.
Jemima collecting musket balls in her apron, as orally told to Eviza.
The Site

The fort today,
in Madison County, Kentucky.

The original Fort Boonesborough did not survive the nineteenth century. The site, however, is preserved. It carries a National Historic Landmark designation from the National Park Service and is administered as Fort Boonesborough State Park by the Kentucky Department of Parks. The park sits on the west bank of the Kentucky River in Madison County, between Richmond and Winchester. A working replica of the fort (built from roughly ten thousand southern yellow pine logs and dedicated in 1974) stands near the original location and operates seasonally from April through October, with resident artisans, cabins, blockhouses, and period demonstrations.

For descendants of the Boonesborough defenders, and for anyone working seriously on the Revolutionary-era frontier, the site is worth visiting. Standing where Rebecca extinguished flaming arrows on the fort roof, and where Jemima slipped out at night to gather spent musket balls, lends a weight to the documentary record that the documents alone cannot carry.

The Draper Correspondence

A granddaughter's
correspondence.

The frontier historian Dr. Lyman Copeland Draper (1815–1891) corresponded with Eviza Lydia Coshow, née Howell, a granddaughter of Jemima Boone Callaway, in an effort to preserve family accounts of the siege before they could be lost. Eviza was the eighth of fourteen children born to Susannah Callaway and Thomas James Howell of Howell's Prairie, St. Charles County, Missouri, and one of approximately eighty grandchildren of Daniel and Rebecca Boone. Despite having sight in only one eye and no formal education, she wrote to Draper at length across 1884 and 1885, relaying her grandmother's accounts as she learned them from first-hand oral history. Her letters are catalogued in the Draper Manuscript Collection, Series C (Kentucky Papers), Volume 21.

The nightly ammunition collection is known almost entirely through this correspondence. Jemima dictated her own memoir later in life, intending to correct inaccuracies in the published record; the manuscript was lost when the riverboat transporting it sank. Draper, for his part, had intended to incorporate Eviza's accounts into a biography of Daniel Boone that he did not live to complete.

"She would go out each night during the siege to collect spent musket balls in her apron, then remold them to use against the enemy the following day."

As told by Jemima Boone Callaway and transmitted through her granddaughter Eviza Lydia Coshow in the Draper Manuscript Collection.

The Role

Another granddaughter,
the same work.

Britta is a direct descendant of both Rebecca Bryan Boone and Jemima Boone Callaway, and one of over 8,000 descendants in the extended Boone family line. Descent does not lower the evidentiary bar. The documentation assembled here satisfies the NSDAR Female Patriot Ancestor standard regardless of who produced it. It does.

Eviza Lydia Coshow (née Howell) was the first granddaughter to do this preservation work, writing to Dr. Lyman Copeland Draper in the mid-1880s to record the accounts her mother Susannah and her grandmother Jemima had told her, before those accounts could be lost. Britta is another, generations later, continuing the same work.

Eviza's letters came to Britta by a fortunate route: through the DAR, a distant Boone cousin, and a regional historical society that held the transcribed correspondence. Reading them reshaped the project. Eviza stopped being another name on a lineage chart and became a real person. Her letters carry the family's presence in a way no summary of the archive can.

This project has taken two forms. The first, "The Eviza Coshow Letters," appeared in Compass Magazine (The Boone Society) in July 2023. The second, three years on, was a live presentation for America 250, delivered to a local DAR chapter in March 2026 and expanding the earlier research into a broader treatment of Rebecca and Jemima as documented NSDAR Female Patriot Ancestors. The presentation matters differently than the article. It places a descendant in the room, speaking on behalf of two patriots who cannot attend their own commemoration.
