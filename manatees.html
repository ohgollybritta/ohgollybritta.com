Welcome to Mossbottom

On the houseboat

Meet Barnaby

Mossbottom is a sleepy little swamp town tucked beneath the cypress trees and Spanish moss. Barnaby, a gentle manatee in a mustard cardigan, lives there on a little houseboat with a crackling woodstove, a record player and a jon boat tied up outside. His family’s old portrait keeps watch from the wall. He has a good book and nowhere to be.

Whiskers, his cat, keeps lookout from the bow and naps by the fire. Tuck, the cutest turtle on the river, moves surprisingly fast for cantaloupe. And if you watch the boat long enough, Rupert the rabbit peeks out to see what’s biting.

The backstory

Why I slow down

This is a passion project for me because I grew up active on the St. Johns River. My dad had a boat, and we always looked out for the safety of our manatee friends.

I’ve also always loved going fast. Car, boat, jet ski, it doesn’t matter. So picture me at five, stuck in a really long no wake zone, impatient to go fast again. That’s when my dad explained what the signs were for: they protected the manatees.

He was right. Florida declared the whole state a manatee refuge and sanctuary in 1978, and that law is what lets the state set slow zones, including stretches of the St. Johns. Going slow gives the boater more time to see a manatee, gives the manatee more time to move, and helps make injuries less severe if they do meet. A 2007 review by state biologists tied about one in four documented manatee deaths in Florida to boats.

Visiting Blue Spring in February to see them was a highlight of growing up in Florida. The park counted about 36 manatees when research began in the 1970s, and more than 700 in 2023. Today I’m an adoptive parent to one of them.

My adopted manatee

Meet Deep Dent

The real manatee who inspired Barnaby. I adopted him through Save the Manatee Club.

Adopt a manatee

Deep Dent was already grown when he first turned up at Blue Spring State Park, near Orange City, in the winter of 1979. The spring stays 72°F all year, warm when the St. Johns runs cold, and he has come back for many winters since. Last winter he logged 16 visits and left on February 7, 2026, the first of the Club’s adoptees to go.

A propeller left a deep dent in his tail, which is how he got his name. He has a similar one on his head, and in the summer of 2010 he came away with another in his lower back and lost a section of his tail. Those scars are how researchers know him on sight.

He’s a little bit shy. When researchers paddle out for the morning count, he’ll sometimes slip to the far bank to dodge the canoe. He also has wanderlust: he has skipped five winters at Blue Spring, three in a row, and turned up as far south as the Riviera Beach power plant in Palm Beach County.

Manatee manners

Admire from a distance

  • Give them space. Never feed, chase or disturb one. They’re a protected species, and it’s against the law.
  • Obey posted manatee zones. “Idle speed, no wake” means only fast enough to steer and keep moving.
  • Watch for snouts, backs, tails and smooth circles on the surface. Those are manatee footprints.
  • Wear polarized glasses, steer around seagrass and shallows, and recycle old fishing line.
