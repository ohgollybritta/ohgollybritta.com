On the houseboat

Meet Barnaby

Mossbottom is inspired by Moss Bluff, on the Ocklawaha River: a storybook fish camp of tea-colored water, bald cypress, Spanish moss and jon boats.

Barnaby is a gentle gray manatee, once a mapmaker, who lives on a faded teal houseboat with Whiskers, his little black cat. He’s never without his mustard cardigan. His mother made it for him.

Her name was Dottie. She and his father, Claude, were river sweethearts, and every winter of their lives they floated to Blue Spring side by side. They raised Barnaby on slow mornings, old jazz records and the patience to let the one that got away go.

Then one winter, on the way to the spring, a boat came through the manatee zone far too fast. Dottie was lost.

Claude survived, with scars across his back that he never talks about. He still lives at Blue Spring, quiet and a little gruff. Barnaby visits him every winter. Claude barely says a word, but he always saves Barnaby the warmest spot in the spring.

On the wall by the woodstove hangs the last picture of the three of them, brown as an old photograph except for the pink in their cheeks. Dottie has one flipper, Claude has the other, and little Barnaby stands between them in a tiny cable-knit cardigan. It’s how he keeps them close. It’s why his jon boat never leaves idle speed, and why he waves every boater down to a crawl.

Raised on the same river

The manatees I grew up with are well traveled. They’re West Indian manatees, a species found from the southern United States through the Caribbean to northern South America, and Florida has its own branch of the family. In the warm months, some head up the coast to Georgia and the Carolinas, others go west to Louisiana, and a few have made it as far as Texas and Massachusetts.

Winter always brings them back. For their size, they carry very little body fat, and they can’t stay in water below 68 degrees for long. So before the worst of the cold, they make their way to Florida’s warm water, often to the very same places year after year. One of those places is Blue Spring, on the St. Johns, where the water stays 72 degrees.

I grew up active on the St. Johns. My dad had a boat, and I have always loved going fast: car, boat, jet ski, it made no difference. What I did not love was a no wake zone. At age five, I was in the middle of a very long one, impatient to pick up speed again, when my dad explained what the zones were for: protecting the manatees.

From then on, I was looking for them, too. By fourteen, I was out on the St. Johns on my own jet ski, and the lesson stuck. Before I opened the throttle, I read the water: smooth circles on the surface, a snout coming up for air, a shadow in the shallows. I loved the speed, but awareness and compassion for the wildlife always came first.

Visiting Blue Spring in February to see them became a highlight of growing up in Florida. I still love to go fast. I also know exactly where to slow down.

My dad had the law on his side. Florida’s Manatee Sanctuary Act, § 379.2431(2), Fla. Stat., makes the whole state a refuge and sanctuary. Paragraph (d) makes it unlawful to intentionally or negligently annoy, molest, harass, disturb, injure or kill a manatee. Paragraph (g)8. calls for boat-speed rules at Blue Springs Run and the connecting waters of the St. Johns within a mile. Violations are generally a misdemeanor under paragraph (s) and § 379.407(1), and going over a posted limit is a civil infraction under § 327.73(1)(k)2.

At Blue Spring, the park counted about 36 manatees when research began in the 1970s, and more than 700 in 2023.

The manatee behind Barnaby

Barnaby has a real-life counterpart. His name is Deep Dent, and I adopted him through Save the Manatee Club.

He was already grown when he first appeared at Blue Spring State Park in the winter of 1979, and he has returned for many winters since. Last season he logged 16 visits and left on February 7, 2026, the first of the Club’s adoptees to go. Where he went next, no published report says.

His name is his evidence. A propeller left a deep dent in his tail. He carries a similar one on his head, and in the summer of 2010 he came away with a third in his lower back and lost a section of his tail. The Club says they likely all came from boats, and researchers identify him by them.

He is shy, known to slip to the far bank when the research canoe comes through, and he answers to no one’s schedule. He has skipped five winters at Blue Spring, three of them in a row. During at least one, photo identification placed him at the Riviera Beach power plant in Palm Beach County, on the Atlantic coast. Scarred, quiet and still going his own way.

Around the next bend

A children’s book is in the works. The working title is Barnaby and the Slow Water, and it’s where we meet Barnaby’s other friends: Rupert, Professor Plume, Miss Rosetta, Miles, Tuck and the rest of the river. It begins with why school matters and grows into why being informed matters, because the dangers animals face are easier to prevent once you understand them.

Until then, I’m participating in the annual Save the Manatee 5K.

Temperament

They call until they find each other.

When a manatee mother and her calf are separated, one or both will call, over and over, until they reunite.

She knows your voice

Young calves make a high squeak that is unique to each one. A mother can pick out her own calf’s call, even in water too murky to see through.

Two years at her side

A calf starts nursing within hours of being born and may nurse for up to two years. She teaches it where to eat, where to rest and how to find the warm water.

Room for one more

It’s rare, but mothers have taken in orphans that weren’t theirs. In Crystal River, researchers watched one nurse and care for a calf that had been trying to feed from strangers.

They feel the whole river

Fine hairs all over the body, and stiff whiskers around the mouth, sense changes in the current and the wake of whatever is near. Their nimble lips work a little like the trunk of an elephant, a distant relative.

Curious, and not shy about it

At Blue Spring, some will follow the alligators around while the alligators try to get away. In the water they roll, swim upside down and turn somersaults.

Built for peace

No biting teeth, only molars for grinding plants. They rest 2 to 12 hours a day, and on a long dive the heart slows to about 30 beats a minute.

The human threat

They have no natural predators.

Nothing in the wild hunts a manatee. The danger is a propeller.

4%of the adult manatees in a ten-year state study had no scars from a boat.

One in four carried the marks of ten or more strikes. In 2025, collisions caused at least 98 deaths, a quarter of the manatees examined, and a record 33 were rescued alive with boat wounds.

Deep Dent’s scars are how researchers know him on sight. A boat is how Barnaby lost his mother.

Hunger
Poor water quality helped wipe out seagrass in the Indian River Lagoon. From December 2020 through April 2022, 1,255 manatees were found dead on the east coast.
Crowding
Disturbed at a warm spring, they can flee into water cold enough to kill them.
Lost habitat
Fouled water and shrinking habitat take away the places they feed, rest and stay warm.
Entanglement
Crab trap line can wrap around a flipper and cut it away.
Litter
Fishing line, nets, hooks and trash wrap, cut and choke.
On the water

Manners for manatees

  • Give them room.Never feed, chase or disturb one. They’re a protected species, and it’s against the law.
  • Obey the zone.“Idle speed, no wake” means only fast enough to steer and keep moving.
  • Read the water.Watch for snouts, backs, tails and smooth circles on the surface. Those are manatee footprints.
  • Come prepared.Wear polarized glasses, steer around seagrass and shallows, and recycle old fishing line.
