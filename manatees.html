The backstory

Why I slow down

This is a passion project for me because I grew up active on the St. Johns River. My dad had a boat, and we always looked out for the safety of our manatee friends.

I’ve also always loved going fast. Car, boat, jet ski, it doesn’t matter. So picture me at five, stuck in a really long no wake zone, impatient to go fast again. That’s when my dad explained what the signs were for: they protected the manatees.

Visiting Blue Spring in February to see them was a highlight of growing up in Florida.

No wake zones

What my dad was talking about

In 1978, the Manatee Sanctuary Act declared all of Florida a refuge and sanctuary for manatees, and it lets the state set the slow zones boaters see on signs. “Idle speed, no wake” means going only fast enough to steer and keep moving.

On the St. Johns, the rules cover stretches of the river around Volusia, Putnam, Lake and Seminole counties. Some zones are year-round and some are seasonal.

Why it helps

A few more seconds

  • The boater has more time to spot a manatee.
  • The manatee has more time to move.
  • If they do meet, the injuries tend to be less severe.

A 2007 review by state biologists tied about one in four documented manatee deaths in Florida to boats.

The real Barnaby

Meet Deep Dent

Barnaby is inspired by a real manatee. Deep Dent is my adopted manatee through Save the Manatee Club. He was already grown when he first turned up at Blue Spring State Park, near Orange City, in the winter of 1979, and he has come back for many winters since.

He was there last winter too, logging 16 visits before heading out on February 7, 2026, the first of the Club’s adoptees to leave.

The scars

How he got his name

A propeller left a deep dent in his tail, and he has a similar one on his head. In the summer of 2010 he came away with another in his lower back and lost a section of his tail.

Those scars are how researchers know him on sight. They’re also the best reason to slow down.

1979

The winter he was first identified. His mother is unknown.

5 winters

Seasons he has skipped at Blue Spring, three of them in a row.

72°F

Blue Spring’s water all year, warm when the St. Johns River runs cold.

Personality

A little bit shy

When researchers paddle out for the early morning count, he’ll sometimes slip over to the far bank to dodge the canoe. Other days he doesn’t mind at all. He’s often on his own, though he has been seen coming and going with Lenny, Floyd and Lucille.

Travels

Quiet, with wanderlust

During at least one missing season, researchers matched him in a photo database to the Riviera Beach power plant in Palm Beach County. In 2014–15 he surprised everyone by swimming all the way up to the boil, where the spring rises.

Blue Spring State Park

From about 36 manatees to more than 700

That’s the park’s count from when research began in the 1970s to 2023. It’s one of the largest winter gathering places for manatees in Florida, and you can watch them from a boardwalk without ever getting in their way.

1,000 lb

Roughly what an adult weighs, at 9 to 10 feet long. Some pass 3,500 pounds.

8 hours

How long they can graze in a day, on seagrasses and other water plants.

68°F

When the water drops below this, they head for springs and other warm water.

Manatee manners

Admire from a distance

  • Give them plenty of space. Binoculars are a good way to get closer.
  • Never feed, chase or disturb one. They’re a protected species, and it’s against the law.
  • Leave resting manatees be, especially in winter. One that flees a warm spring can end up in dangerously cold water.
  • See a tag near the tail? Leave it on. Researchers are keeping an eye on that one.
On the water

Look for footprints

  • Watch for snouts, backs, tails and smooth circles on the surface. Those circles are manatee footprints.
  • Obey posted manatee zones and slow down. Boat strikes are a major threat.
  • Wear polarized glasses and pick someone to be the lookout.
  • Steer around seagrass and shallow areas, and recycle old fishing line.
FWC Wildlife Alert Hotline

See one that needs help?

888-404-3922

Call about a sick, injured, orphaned, tangled or dead manatee in Florida. Note the exact location, the nearest boat ramp, its size and whether it has a tag. Take photos or video, and don’t try to handle it yourself.

Little wonders

Built different

  • They’re distant relatives of the elephant.
  • They can’t turn their heads sideways, so they turn their whole body.
  • Their teeth never stop being replaced, from back to front.
