Welcome to Mossbottom

An ambient YouTube channel set in a storybook Old Florida river town, where the porch lights stay on and somebody’s always got a record playing. Gentle creatures. Slow jazz. Nowhere to be.

On the houseboat

Meet Barnaby

Barnaby is a gentle gray manatee, once a mapmaker, who lives on a faded teal houseboat with Whiskers, his little black cat. He’s never without his mustard cardigan. His mother made it for him.

Her name was Dottie. She and his father, Claude, were river sweethearts, and every winter of their lives they floated to Blue Spring side by side. They raised Barnaby on slow mornings, old jazz records and the patience to let the one that got away go.

Then one winter, on the way to the spring, a boat came through the manatee zone far too fast. Dottie was lost.

Claude survived, with scars across his back that he never talks about. He still lives at Blue Spring, quiet and a little gruff. Barnaby visits him every winter. Claude barely says a word, but he always saves Barnaby the warmest spot in the spring.

Look for the portrait by the woodstove in the videos. Two grown manatees, side by side, and little Barnaby in front, each parent holding one of his flippers. It’s the last one of him with his entire family, and it’s how he keeps them both close. It’s also why he putters along at idle speed and waves every boater down to a crawl.

Why I slow down

Once there was a girl who grew up on the St. Johns River, and all she ever wanted was to go fast. By car, by boat, by anything with a motor, as long as the wind was loud.

But there was one long, long stretch of river where her father always eased the boat down to a whisper. No wake, said the signs, and on and on they crept, while the girl fidgeted and sighed and waited for the fast part.

So her father told her the secret of the slow water. Somewhere beneath them, big and gentle and in no hurry at all, were the manatees. The signs were there to keep them safe.

After that, she kept a lookout. And every February, when the river ran cold, she went to the spring where the water stays warm, and there they were, all her quiet neighbors, waiting out the winter.

The girl grew up. She still loves to go fast. But she never forgot the slow water, and one day she built a little town there and called it Mossbottom.

The slow water is real. Florida became a manatee refuge and sanctuary by law in 1978, and that law is why there are slow zones on the St. Johns today. Going slow gives a boater more time to see a manatee and gives the manatee more time to move. At Blue Spring, the park counted about 36 manatees when research began in the 1970s, and more than 700 in 2023.

Who inspired Barnaby

His name is Deep Dent, and I adopted him through Save the Manatee Club. Barnaby is inspired by him.

He was already grown when he first turned up at Blue Spring State Park, near Orange City, in the winter of 1979. The spring stays 72°F all year, warm when the St. Johns runs cold, and he has come back for many winters since. Last winter he logged 16 visits and left on February 7, 2026, the first of the Club’s adoptees to go.

A propeller left a deep dent in his tail, which is how he got his name. He has a similar one on his head, and in the summer of 2010 he came away with another in his lower back and lost a section of his tail. Those scars are how researchers know him on sight.

He’s a little bit shy. When researchers paddle out for the morning count, he’ll sometimes slip to the far bank to dodge the canoe. He also has wanderlust: he has skipped five winters at Blue Spring, three in a row, and turned up as far south as the Riviera Beach power plant in Palm Beach County.

What’s next?

A children’s book is in the works. Why Barnaby Went to School brings the whole river together, from Whiskers and Rupert to Tuck, the oldest soul in Mossbottom. A later story will bring in Claude and Dottie, to teach kids about manatees and safe boating.

I’m also taking part in the Save the Manatee 5K. And if the YouTube channel ever makes revenue, a major share of the proceeds will go toward manatee preservation.

Temperament

Easygoing by nature

  • They spend up to eight hours a day grazing on seagrass and water plants, and rest anywhere from 2 to 12 hours a day.
  • They’re semi-social, just as likely to be alone as in company. Small groups come and go, with no leader and no real herd.
  • A calf may stay with its mother for up to two years.
  • They’re slow-moving, but can reach 15 miles per hour in short bursts.
Threats

What they’re up against

  • Boats. Collisions injure and kill manatees, and some survivors carry the scars.
  • Cold. They can’t tolerate water below 68°F for long. Cold stress can make them very sick, so warm springs matter.
  • Habitat loss. They need places to feed, rest and stay warm.
  • Pollution and algae blooms. Florida’s park service names both as threats.
  • Litter and fishing line. Entangled manatees need trained rescuers.
Manatee manners

Admire from a distance

  • Give them space. Never feed, chase or disturb one. They’re a protected species, and it’s against the law.
  • Obey posted manatee zones. “Idle speed, no wake” means only fast enough to steer and keep moving.
  • Watch for snouts, backs, tails and smooth circles on the surface. Those are manatee footprints.
  • Wear polarized glasses, steer around seagrass and shallows, and recycle old fishing line.
